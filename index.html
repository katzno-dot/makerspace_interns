Welcome to your Makerspace Internship.

You're not a student in a workshop. You're a novice intern in a working makerspace — investigating tools, testing safely, making purposeful things, and building the shared catalogue every future maker will rely on.

The Central Course Question

"How can we learn an unfamiliar tool well enough to use it responsibly — and help someone else begin learning it?"

01 — Course Purpose

By the end of the semester, you should be able to...

The whole course is built to get you to these nine abilities. Every activity, cycle, and rubric line ladders back up to this list.

🧭

Explain what a makerspace is and how makers contribute to a community.

🔍

Approach an unfamiliar tool with curiosity and appropriate caution.

❓

Ask useful questions about function, operation, materials, and safety.

📚

Find and evaluate manuals, labels, demonstrations, and knowledgeable people.

🧪

Conduct safe, structured tests rather than random experimentation.

🛠️

Use evidence from testing to design and make something.

🗣️

Explain a tool clearly to another beginner.

📝

Document your own learning and contribute responsibly to shared work.

💡

Reflect on mistakes, feedback, and improvements.

The class's public product is a shared tool catalogue. Your individual portfolio is the evidence of your own growth.

03 — How Much Is Enough?

Determining the right depth

A catalogue entry doesn't need to teach everything an expert knows. It needs enough for a beginner to get started safely.

A beginner-ready entry helps someone…

  1. Understand what the tool is for.
  2. Recognize its important parts.
  3. Know the questions to ask before using it.
  4. Prepare for a supervised first use.
  5. Avoid predictable hazards and mistakes.
  6. Find more detailed, authoritative information.
THE RULE

Include a detail when it affects safety, material compatibility, setup, operation, the quality of the result, or the ability to troubleshoot.

Example — Drill Press

You wouldn't document every possible bit and speed. You'd explain why bit type, material, speed, workholding, and drilling depth matter — then link to authoritative specs.

05 — Your Evidence

The individual student portfolio

The catalogue is a team product. The portfolio is yours — proof of what you personally learned, even on a shared project. A physical maker notebook + light digital reflections works well.

For every tool cycle, keep:

  • Initial observations and questions
  • A labeled sketch or annotated photograph
  • Research notes and sources
  • Safety understanding
  • Test records
  • Planning evidence
  • Photographs of making
  • Personal contribution to the team
  • Feedback received
  • Changes and improvements
  • Final reflection
